1. Computer Network

Definition

A computer network is a collection of two or more autonomous computing devices interconnected through communication links so that they can exchange data and share resources. The devices may include computers, servers, printers, smartphones, switches and routers. Communication takes place according to agreed rules called protocols.

Basic components

  1. End devices / hosts: Computers, phones, servers and other devices that send or receive information.
  2. Network Interface Card (NIC): Provides a device with an interface to connect to a network.
  3. Transmission medium: The wired or wireless path over which signals travel.
  4. Interconnecting devices: Switches, routers, bridges and access points that connect network segments or networks.
  5. Network software and protocols: Control communication, addressing, delivery and resource sharing.

Objectives and advantages

  • Resource sharing: Sharing printers, storage, applications and internet connections.
  • Communication: Email, messaging, voice calls, video conferencing and file transfer.
  • Information sharing: Access to common databases and centrally stored information.
  • Reliability: Alternative resources or paths can help maintain service when a component fails, depending on network design.
  • Scalability: New devices and services can be added as requirements grow.
  • Cost efficiency: Shared resources can reduce duplication of hardware and services.
  • Centralized management: User accounts, security policies, backups and software can be administered centrally.
Example: In a college computer laboratory, all computers may use a common switch to access a shared server, network printer and internet connection.

2. Types of Networks

2.1 Local Area Network (LAN)

A LAN connects computers and other devices within a limited geographical area such as a room, office, building, laboratory or campus block. It is generally owned, managed and maintained by one organization or individual.

  • Usually offers high data rates and relatively low delay.
  • Commonly uses Ethernet (IEEE 802.3) or Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11).
  • Allows sharing of files, printers, applications and internet access.
  • Example: a college laboratory network or an office floor network.

2.2 Metropolitan Area Network (MAN)

A MAN connects multiple LANs across a city or a large metropolitan region. It covers a larger area than a LAN but is generally smaller than a WAN.

  • May connect offices, campuses, government buildings or branches located across a city.
  • Can be operated by a service provider, public authority or large organization.
  • Often uses high-capacity fibre-optic links or service-provider infrastructure.
  • Example: a network connecting the branches of a bank across Mumbai.

2.3 Wide Area Network (WAN)

A WAN connects networks over large geographical distances, such as across states, countries or continents. It commonly uses infrastructure supplied by telecommunications operators or internet service providers.

  • Connects geographically distant LANs and MANs.
  • Uses technologies such as leased lines, optical fibre, cellular networks and satellite links.
  • Usually involves service-provider infrastructure and routing between networks.
  • Example: a company network connecting offices in Mumbai, Delhi, London and Singapore.

LAN, MAN and WAN — comparison

Parameter LAN MAN WAN
Coverage Room, building or campus area City or metropolitan area Country, continent or global area
Ownership Usually one person or organization May be private or service-provider operated Often uses infrastructure of multiple service providers
Typical performance High speed, low local delay Varies with design and provider Varies with distance and access technology
Cost Usually lower within a limited site Higher than a typical LAN Often higher due to long-distance connectivity
Example Office network City-wide institutional network Global enterprise network

2.4 Wireless Networks

A wireless network transfers information through electromagnetic waves rather than requiring a physical cable for the radio link. Devices communicate using wireless technologies such as Wi-Fi, Bluetooth, cellular networks or satellite communication.

  • Provides mobility and convenient installation.
  • Can connect devices where cabling is difficult or expensive.
  • Performance may be affected by distance, obstacles, interference and shared radio capacity.
  • Requires suitable security controls, such as strong authentication and encryption.

Wireless networks can operate at different scales: a personal-area wireless connection, a Wi-Fi LAN, a metropolitan wireless network or a wide-area cellular network.

3. Network Software, Protocols and Services

3.1 Network software

Network software is the collection of programs and system components that enable devices to communicate and share resources. It includes protocol implementations, network operating-system services, device drivers, applications and management tools.

  • Protocol implementations: Implement communication rules such as Ethernet, IP, TCP and HTTP.
  • Network operating-system services: Provide file sharing, user authentication, directory services and printer sharing.
  • Network applications: Provide user-facing services such as web browsing, email and file transfer.
  • Network management software: Monitors devices, performance, faults and configuration.

3.2 Protocol

A protocol is a set of rules that governs communication between entities in a network. It specifies the format and meaning of messages and the actions taken when messages are sent or received.

Elements of a protocol

  1. Syntax: The structure or format of data, including field order, encoding and message layout.
  2. Semantics: The meaning of each field and the actions required when a message is received.
  3. Timing: When data should be transmitted and at what rate, including sequencing and synchronization requirements.

Why are protocols required?

  • They allow devices made by different manufacturers to communicate.
  • They define how data is formatted, addressed, transmitted and interpreted.
  • They coordinate the sender and receiver and establish communication procedures.
  • They support error handling, flow control and reliable delivery where required.
  • They enable interoperability through standardized communication rules.

Examples of protocols

Protocol Purpose (overview)
HTTP / HTTPS Transfers web resources between clients and web servers.
TCP Provides connection-oriented, reliable byte-stream transport.
IP Provides logical addressing and packet forwarding between networks.
Ethernet Defines wired LAN communication at the data-link and physical layers.
DNS Maps domain names to information such as IP addresses.
Wi-Fi (IEEE 802.11) Defines wireless LAN communication.

3.3 Protocols and services

A service describes what a layer offers to the layer above it. A protocol describes the rules used by peer entities at the same layer to communicate. A layer uses the services of the layer below it and provides services to the layer above it.

Service

What a layer makes available to the next higher layer.

Focus: the interface and functionality offered.

Protocol

How corresponding peer layers exchange control and data information.

Focus: message formats and communication rules.

4. Design Issues for Network Layers

Layered network architecture divides the complex task of communication into smaller functions. While designing layers and protocols, several common issues must be considered.

  1. Addressing: A sender must identify the intended receiver or destination. Different layers may use different forms of addressing.
  2. Error control: Data may be damaged, lost or duplicated. A protocol may detect errors and, where required, recover through acknowledgements or retransmission.
  3. Flow control: A fast sender should not overwhelm a slower receiver. Flow-control mechanisms regulate the amount or rate of data sent.
  4. Sequencing: Data may arrive out of order. Sequence information can help the receiver arrange data in the correct order.
  5. Fragmentation and reassembly: Large messages may need to be divided into smaller units for transmission and reassembled at the destination.
  6. Multiplexing and demultiplexing: Multiple conversations may share a communication facility; the receiver must deliver incoming data to the correct conversation or application.
  7. Routing: In an internetwork, a suitable path must be selected to carry packets toward their destinations.
  8. Connection establishment and release: Some communication services establish state before data transfer and release resources after communication ends.
  9. Quality of service: Some applications require particular performance characteristics, such as bounded delay, throughput or loss.
  10. Security: Communication may require confidentiality, integrity, authentication and access control.
Exam point: The exact set of functions implemented at a layer depends on the architecture and service being provided. Do not assume every layer performs every function.

5. OSI Reference Model

Introduction

The Open Systems Interconnection (OSI) reference model, developed by the International Organization for Standardization (ISO), is a seven-layer conceptual framework used to describe and standardize the functions involved in network communication. Each layer performs a defined set of functions, provides services to the layer above it and uses services from the layer below it.

7. ApplicationNetwork services for user applications
6. PresentationTranslation, format, compression, encryption
5. SessionDialog control and synchronization
4. TransportEnd-to-end delivery, segmentation, reliability
3. NetworkLogical addressing and routing
2. Data LinkFraming, link addressing and error detection
1. PhysicalTransmission of raw bits over the medium

Figure: Seven layers of the OSI reference model, from the user-facing Application layer to the transmission-oriented Physical layer.

Functions of the seven layers

1. Physical Layer

The Physical layer is the lowest layer. It transmits raw bits over a physical communication medium.

  • Defines electrical, optical, radio and mechanical characteristics of the interface and medium.
  • Specifies signal representation, bit timing, data rate and physical connectors.
  • Defines transmission mode and physical topology-related characteristics.
  • Deals with the activation, maintenance and deactivation of the physical link.

Examples: cables, connectors, radio transmission and physical signalling specifications.

2. Data Link Layer

The Data Link layer provides node-to-node delivery over a single link and organizes bits into frames.

  • Framing: divides the bit stream into manageable frames.
  • Physical / link addressing: identifies devices on the local link where applicable.
  • Error detection and, in some protocols, error correction or retransmission.
  • Flow control between directly connected nodes where supported.
  • Media access control on shared communication media.

Examples: Ethernet framing and the MAC sublayer of IEEE 802.11.

3. Network Layer

The Network layer is responsible for delivering packets across multiple interconnected networks.

  • Logical addressing, such as IP addressing.
  • Routing and path selection.
  • Packet forwarding between networks.
  • Internetworking and, depending on the protocol, fragmentation-related functions.

Example: IP. Routers primarily operate at this layer for packet forwarding.

4. Transport Layer

The Transport layer provides communication between processes running on end systems.

  • Segmentation and reassembly of application data.
  • Process-to-process delivery using port numbers in transport protocols such as TCP and UDP.
  • Connection management.
  • Reliability, sequencing, acknowledgements and retransmission when provided by the protocol.
  • Flow control and congestion control in protocols that support them.

Examples: TCP and UDP.

5. Session Layer

The Session layer establishes, manages and terminates logical sessions between communicating applications.

  • Dialog control, including coordination of communication.
  • Synchronization through checkpoints in long data exchanges.
  • Session establishment, maintenance and termination.

6. Presentation Layer

The Presentation layer deals with the representation of information exchanged between systems.

  • Data translation between different formats or character representations.
  • Data compression and decompression.
  • Encryption and decryption as presentation-related functions in the model.

7. Application Layer

The Application layer provides network services to application processes and acts as the interface between network functions and user-facing software.

  • Supports services such as web access, file transfer, email and name resolution.
  • Defines application-level message exchanges and service requests.

Examples: HTTP, FTP, DNS and SMTP are commonly associated with this layer in the OSI model.

Data encapsulation in the OSI model

At the sender, each layer passes data to the layer below and may add its own control information. At the receiver, the corresponding layers process and remove that information in reverse order. This is called encapsulation at the sender and decapsulation at the receiver.

Application data→Segment→Packet→Frame→Bits

Simplified data-unit progression while moving down the stack. Exact protocol data-unit names vary by layer and protocol.

6. TCP/IP Reference Model

Introduction

The TCP/IP model is the protocol architecture used by the Internet. It organizes communication functions into layers and is based on the protocols that enable communication across interconnected networks.

ApplicationHTTP, FTP, DNS, SMTP
TransportTCP, UDP
InternetIP, ICMP
Link / Network AccessEthernet, Wi-Fi and physical access technologies

Four-layer TCP/IP model used in this comparison. Some textbooks present a five-layer teaching model by separating the physical and data-link functions.

Functions of TCP/IP layers

  1. Application layer: Provides network services to application programs. It includes application protocols such as HTTP, FTP, DNS and SMTP. It combines functions represented by the OSI Application, Presentation and Session layers.
  2. Transport layer: Provides end-to-end communication between application processes. TCP offers a reliable, connection-oriented byte stream; UDP provides connectionless datagram delivery.
  3. Internet layer: Handles logical addressing and packet forwarding across interconnected networks. IP is the central protocol of this layer.
  4. Link / Network Access layer: Handles delivery over the local network link and the interface with the transmission medium. Technologies include Ethernet and Wi-Fi.

OSI and TCP/IP — comparison

Basis OSI model TCP/IP model
Origin Reference model developed by ISO. Protocol architecture developed around the TCP/IP protocol suite.
Number of layers Seven layers. Four layers in the common model shown here.
Upper layers Application, Presentation and Session are separate. These functions are grouped in the Application layer.
Lower layers Data Link and Physical are separate. Combined in the Link / Network Access layer in the four-layer model.
Network layer service Can describe connection-oriented and connectionless services conceptually. Internet layer is based on connectionless IP datagrams.
Protocol dependence Protocol-independent reference framework. Closely associated with the TCP/IP protocol suite.
Primary use Useful for learning, design and describing network functions. Forms the practical architecture of the Internet.
Important distinction: OSI is a reference model; TCP/IP is both a protocol suite and the architecture commonly used to explain Internet communication. Do not describe the OSI model as the protocol used by the Internet.

7. Network Topologies

Network topology refers to the physical or logical arrangement of nodes and communication links in a network. Physical topology describes how devices and cables are arranged; logical topology describes how data moves between devices.

7.1 Bus topology

All devices share a common main cable called the backbone. Data transmitted on the bus is available to connected devices, and the intended receiver accepts the frame.

  • Advantages: Simple layout, less cable than some alternatives, low initial cost for small networks.
  • Disadvantages: Backbone failure can stop the entire network; performance can degrade as traffic increases; fault isolation can be difficult; cable length and node count are limited.

7.2 Star topology

Every device has a separate point-to-point connection to a central device, usually a switch or hub.

  • Advantages: Easy to install, expand and troubleshoot; failure of one end-device cable usually affects only that device.
  • Disadvantages: Failure of the central device can disrupt the network; requires more cable than a bus layout.

7.3 Ring topology

Each device is connected to two neighboring devices, forming a closed loop. Data travels around the ring, in one direction or in both directions depending on the technology.

  • Advantages: Orderly access can be provided; predictable transmission behaviour in some ring systems.
  • Disadvantages: A break or failed node can disrupt a simple unprotected ring; adding or removing devices may interrupt operation.

7.4 Mesh topology

Devices are interconnected through multiple links. In a full mesh, every node has a direct link to every other node. A partial mesh connects only selected node pairs.

  • Advantages: Multiple paths improve redundancy and fault tolerance; a single link failure may not disconnect the network.
  • Disadvantages: Full mesh requires many links and ports; installation and management can be expensive and complex.

7.5 Tree topology

A tree topology organizes devices in a hierarchy, often by connecting multiple star networks to a central backbone or higher-level device.

  • Advantages: Scalable and suitable for hierarchical expansion; faults can sometimes be isolated to a branch.
  • Disadvantages: Failure of a higher-level device or backbone can affect a large part of the network; cabling and configuration are more complex.

7.6 Hybrid topology

A hybrid topology combines two or more topology types, such as star-bus or star-ring. It can be designed to meet the needs of a large organization.

  • Advantages: Flexible, scalable and adaptable to different departments or locations.
  • Disadvantages: Design, implementation and troubleshooting may be complex and costly.
Topology Structure Main strength Main limitation
Bus Shared backbone Simple and economical for small layouts Backbone failure affects all nodes
Star Central switch / hub Easy management and fault isolation Central device is a critical point
Ring Closed loop Orderly access in suitable ring systems Break may disrupt a simple ring

8. Transmission Media

Definition

Transmission media are the physical paths or channels through which data signals travel from a sender to a receiver. They are broadly classified into guided (wired) and unguided (wireless) media.

Guided media

Signals travel through a physical path such as copper cable or optical fibre.

Examples: twisted-pair cable, coaxial cable, optical fibre.

Unguided media

Signals propagate through free space using electromagnetic waves.

Examples: radio waves, microwaves, infrared and satellite links.

8.1 Guided transmission media

A. Twisted-pair cable

Twisted-pair cable consists of two insulated copper wires twisted around each other. Twisting helps reduce electromagnetic interference and crosstalk. It is widely used in telephone systems and Ethernet LAN cabling.

  • UTP (Unshielded Twisted Pair): Does not have additional metallic shielding around the pairs; widely used in LANs and relatively inexpensive.
  • STP (Shielded Twisted Pair): Includes shielding to reduce interference; typically costs more and requires proper grounding and installation.
  • Advantages: Low cost, flexible, easy to install and widely available.
  • Limitations: More susceptible to noise and attenuation than fibre; distance and performance depend on cable category and installation.

B. Coaxial cable

Coaxial cable has a central copper conductor surrounded by an insulating dielectric, a metallic shield and an outer protective jacket. The conductor and shield share a common axis, giving the cable its name.

  • Advantages: Better shielding against external interference than basic unshielded twisted pair; suitable for radio-frequency signals.
  • Limitations: Bulkier and less flexible than twisted pair; installation and connectors can be more involved.
  • Applications: Cable television, broadband access and some radio-frequency systems.

C. Optical fibre

Optical fibre carries information as pulses of light through a thin glass or plastic fibre. Its main parts are the core, cladding and protective coating. Light is guided along the core by the optical properties of the core and cladding.

  • Single-mode fibre: Has a very small core and supports one principal propagation mode; suitable for high-capacity, long-distance links.
  • Multimode fibre: Has a larger core and supports multiple propagation modes; commonly used over shorter distances.
  • Advantages: High bandwidth, low attenuation over long distances, immunity to electromagnetic interference and electrical isolation.
  • Limitations: Requires optical transceivers and specialized joining/testing equipment; fibre can be damaged by excessive bending or poor handling.
  • Applications: Internet backbone, data centres, submarine cables and fibre-to-the-home networks.

8.2 Unguided transmission media

A. Radio waves

Radio waves can propagate over broad areas and may penetrate some obstacles. They are used in broadcasting, Wi-Fi, Bluetooth and cellular communication. Their behaviour depends on frequency, power, antenna and environment.

B. Microwaves

Microwave communication commonly uses directional antennas and often requires a clear line of sight between stations. It is used in point-to-point terrestrial links and satellite communication.

C. Infrared

Infrared communication is commonly used for short-range links and generally does not pass through opaque walls. It can be useful where communication is confined to a room or a limited area.

D. Satellite communication

A satellite acts as a relay station: it receives an uplink signal, processes or amplifies it, and retransmits it on a downlink frequency. Satellite links provide wide geographical coverage but may introduce propagation delay, particularly with geostationary satellites.

Guided and unguided media — comparison

Basis Guided media Unguided media
Transmission path Physical cable or fibre guides the signal. Signal propagates through air, vacuum or space.
Examples Twisted pair, coaxial cable, optical fibre. Radio, microwave, infrared, satellite.
Mobility Devices are physically connected to the medium. Can support mobility and wireless access.
Interference Depends on medium; fibre is immune to electromagnetic interference. May be affected by interference, obstacles and shared spectrum.

9. Network Architectures: Client-Server and Peer-to-Peer

9.1 Client-server architecture

In a client-server architecture, one or more server computers provide services or resources, and client computers request and use those services. The server listens for requests, processes them and returns responses.

Characteristics

  • Servers provide centralized services and resources.
  • Clients initiate requests to access server resources.
  • Administration, backup and security policies can be centrally managed.
  • Servers may be scaled or replicated to handle increased demand.

Advantages

  • Centralized data and administration.
  • Improved control over access and permissions.
  • Convenient backup and maintenance.
  • Suitable for organizations requiring shared services.

Disadvantages

  • Server failure can interrupt the services it provides unless redundancy is designed.
  • Requires server hardware, administration and maintenance.
  • Heavy demand may overload a server or network link.

Examples

Web browsing (browser and web server), email services, database applications and centralized file servers.

9.2 Peer-to-peer (P2P) architecture

In a peer-to-peer architecture, participating computers (peers) can communicate and share resources directly. A peer may act as both a client and a provider of resources. A dedicated central server is not required for every exchange, although some P2P systems use coordination or indexing servers.

Characteristics

  • Peers can share files, processing capacity or other resources directly.
  • Each peer may request and provide services.
  • Management can be distributed among participants.
  • Performance and availability may depend on participating peers.

Advantages

  • Can avoid dependence on one central service host for every exchange.
  • Resources and workload can be distributed among peers.
  • Can be economical for suitable small or distributed sharing tasks.

Disadvantages

  • Security, access control and backup can be harder to manage consistently.
  • Availability depends on peers being online and sharing resources.
  • Management and data consistency can become difficult as the system grows.

Client-server vs peer-to-peer

Basis Client-server Peer-to-peer
Organization Central server provides services to clients. Peers share resources directly and may act as both requester and provider.
Control Generally centralized. Generally distributed.
Management Central administration is easier. Administration is distributed and may be harder.
Failure impact Server failure may affect dependent services. Failure of one peer generally affects that peer's contribution; availability depends on system design.
Security Policies can be enforced centrally. Consistent security enforcement can be more difficult.
Examples Web application with a web server; centralized file server. Direct file sharing and distributed content-sharing systems.

10. Network Devices

10.1 Bridge

A bridge is a data-link-layer device used to connect LAN segments. It examines MAC addresses and learns which devices are reachable through which ports. It forwards or filters frames based on its forwarding information.

  • Operates primarily at OSI Layer 2.
  • Learns source MAC addresses from incoming frames.
  • Forwards frames toward the segment containing the destination, when known.
  • Can reduce unnecessary traffic between network segments.

10.2 Switch

A network switch is a multiport device that connects devices within a LAN. A typical Ethernet switch operates at the Data Link layer and forwards frames using a MAC address table.

  • Learns source MAC addresses and associates them with switch ports.
  • Forwards known unicast frames to the appropriate port.
  • Floods certain frames, such as broadcasts and unknown unicasts, within the relevant VLAN.
  • Each switch port creates a separate collision domain in typical full-duplex Ethernet operation.
  • Supports efficient communication among many LAN devices.

10.3 Router

A router connects different IP networks and forwards packets between them. It examines destination IP addresses and uses a routing table to select a next hop or outgoing interface.

  • Operates primarily at OSI Layer 3.
  • Connects separate networks, such as a home LAN and an ISP network.
  • Uses static routes or routing protocols to learn routes.
  • Separates broadcast domains by default.

10.4 Gateway

A gateway is a device or software function that provides an entry or exit point between networks or systems. In the broad sense, a gateway may connect networks that use different protocols or architectures and may perform protocol translation. In common IP networking, the term default gateway usually refers to the router (or Layer 3 device) to which a host sends traffic destined for other networks.

10.5 Access Point (AP)

A wireless access point provides Wi-Fi devices with access to a wired LAN. It bridges wireless clients onto the local network and manages wireless association and radio communication.

  • Allows wireless devices to join a network.
  • Connects wireless stations to the wired distribution network.
  • Provides wireless network identification and security mechanisms.
  • Multiple access points can be deployed to extend wireless coverage.

10.6 Hub

A hub is a basic multiport repeater that operates at the Physical layer. It repeats incoming electrical signals to its other ports rather than examining MAC or IP addresses.

  • Does not learn MAC addresses or make forwarding decisions.
  • All connected devices share the same collision domain.
  • Typically operates in half-duplex environments and is largely obsolete in modern Ethernet LANs.

11. Self-learning Topics

11.1 Encoding

Encoding is the process of representing information in a form suitable for storage, processing or transmission. In data communication, digital data is represented by signal patterns so that a receiver can recover the transmitted bits.

Line encoding maps a sequence of bits to signal elements. Common conceptual categories include:

  • Unipolar encoding: Uses one polarity for one signal state and a reference level for the other.
  • Polar encoding: Uses positive and negative voltage levels to represent signal states. Examples include NRZ-L, NRZ-I and Manchester encoding.
  • Bipolar encoding: Uses three levels, commonly positive, zero and negative. Alternate Mark Inversion (AMI) is an example.

Encoding schemes are evaluated based on synchronization, bandwidth requirement, baseline wandering, DC components and error-detection properties. Detailed line-code waveforms are not expanded here because the uploaded syllabus lists encodings as a self-learning topic without specifying a required set of schemes.

11.2 Frequency Hopping Spread Spectrum (FHSS)

FHSS is a spread-spectrum technique in which the transmitter rapidly changes its carrier frequency among a set of frequency channels according to a predetermined hopping sequence shared with the receiver.

  1. The available frequency band is divided into multiple channels.
  2. The transmitter and receiver agree on a hopping sequence and timing.
  3. The transmitter sends data on one channel for a short interval.
  4. Both sides hop to the next selected frequency according to the sequence.
  5. The receiver follows the same sequence to recover the data.

Potential benefits: resistance to narrowband interference and reduced likelihood that a persistent interferer will disrupt every hop. Both ends must remain synchronized.

11.3 Direct Sequence Spread Spectrum (DSSS)

DSSS spreads a data signal over a wider frequency band by combining the data with a higher-rate spreading code (often called a chip sequence). The receiver uses the corresponding code to despread and recover the original information.

  1. The sender takes the original data bits.
  2. Each data bit is combined with a faster spreading-code sequence.
  3. The resulting signal occupies a wider bandwidth than the original data signal.
  4. The receiver correlates the received signal with the same spreading code to recover the data.

Potential benefits: processing gain against certain forms of interference and the ability to distinguish signals using different spreading codes in systems designed for that purpose.

FHSS and DSSS — comparison

Basis FHSS DSSS
Spreading method Changes carrier frequency according to a hopping sequence. Multiplies / combines data with a high-rate spreading code.
Signal behaviour Uses different frequency channels at different times. Spreads signal energy over a wider band at the same time.
Synchronization Transmitter and receiver must follow the same hopping pattern and timing. Receiver must know and synchronize to the spreading code.
Interference response Can avoid a persistently interfered channel by hopping away. Can provide processing gain against some narrowband interference.
