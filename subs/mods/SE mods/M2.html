Requirements Engineering and Modelling

Requirements engineering establishes what a software system must do, the constraints under which it must operate, and the models used to understand and communicate the proposed system.

02.1 Requirements Engineering

Definition: Requirements engineering (RE) is the systematic process of discovering, analysing, documenting, validating and managing the services and constraints required from a software system.

It acts as a bridge between the customer's needs and the technical solution developed by the software team. It reduces misunderstanding by making requirements explicit and verifiable.

Objectives

  • Understand the needs and expectations of stakeholders.
  • Define the system's scope and boundaries.
  • Identify functional and non-functional requirements.
  • Resolve conflicts and ambiguities among requirements.
  • Provide a documented basis for design, implementation and testing.
Exam point: Requirements engineering is not limited to writing an SRS. It includes elicitation, analysis, specification, validation and ongoing requirements management.

02.2 Activities of Requirements Engineering

The activities are commonly performed iteratively. Feedback from validation or changing stakeholder needs can send the process back to an earlier activity.

Conceptual flow of requirements engineering. Validation may cause requirements to be revisited; management continues throughout the project.

1. Inception

Establishes the basic understanding of the problem, stakeholders, business goals, system scope and feasibility.

2. Elicitation

Collects requirements from stakeholders and other sources using interviews, observation, workshops, questionnaires and document study.

3. Elaboration / Analysis

Organises and refines collected information, develops models, identifies conflicts, checks feasibility and prioritises requirements.

4. Negotiation

Resolves conflicting stakeholder expectations and agrees on a realistic set of requirements within cost, schedule and technical constraints.

5. Specification

Documents the agreed requirements in a structured form, commonly in a Software Requirement Specification (SRS).

6. Validation

Checks that requirements describe the intended system, are consistent, complete, feasible, unambiguous and testable.

7. Requirements Management

Controls requirement versions, changes, priorities, dependencies and traceability throughout the software life cycle.

02.3 Requirements Elicitation

Definition: Requirements elicitation is the process of gathering information about the required system from customers, users, domain experts and other stakeholders.

Common elicitation techniques

TechniqueDescriptionUse
InterviewDirect discussion with stakeholders using structured or open-ended questions.Detailed understanding and clarification.
QuestionnaireA prepared set of questions distributed to many users.Collecting broad feedback efficiently.
ObservationWatching users perform their actual tasks in the working environment.Discovering implicit and practical needs.
Workshop / JADFacilitated sessions involving users, customers and developers.Building shared understanding and resolving differences.
Document analysisStudying existing forms, manuals, policies and legacy-system documents.Understanding existing processes and constraints.
PrototypingCreating an early representation of the system for stakeholder feedback.Clarifying unclear interface or workflow requirements.

Challenges in elicitation

  • Stakeholders may not know exactly what they need.
  • Different stakeholders may have conflicting expectations.
  • Requirements may be incomplete, ambiguous or inconsistent.
  • Communication gaps may arise because users and developers use different terminology.
  • Requirements may change as business conditions evolve.

02.4 Requirements Analysis

Requirements analysis examines elicited requirements to determine whether they are necessary, feasible, complete, consistent and sufficiently precise.

Main analysis tasks

  • Classification: group requirements into categories such as functional, non-functional and domain requirements.
  • Prioritisation: rank requirements according to business value, urgency, risk and cost.
  • Conflict resolution: identify and negotiate incompatible requirements.
  • Modelling: represent requirements using use-case, class, activity or behavioural models.
  • Feasibility analysis: check technical, operational, economic and schedule feasibility.
Remember: Elicitation discovers requirements; analysis refines, classifies, prioritises and checks them.

02.5 Types of Requirements

Functional requirements

Describe the services, tasks or behaviours that the system must provide.

Example: The library system shall allow a member to search for a book by title.

Non-functional requirements

Specify quality attributes, performance targets or constraints on how the system operates.

Example: Search results shall be displayed within two seconds for 95% of requests under the stated load.

Domain requirements

Arise from the application domain, its rules, terminology, standards or regulations.

Example: A banking system must apply the interest calculation rules defined by the bank.

Interface / constraint requirements

Specify required interactions with external systems, hardware, users or mandated technologies.

Example: The application shall exchange payment status through the approved payment gateway API.

Functional vs Non-functional requirements

FunctionalNon-functional
Defines what the system does.Defines how well or under what constraints it operates.
Usually expressed as system services or behaviours.Often expressed as measurable quality targets or restrictions.
Example: user can reset a password.Example: password reset confirmation is sent within 30 seconds.

02.6 Software Requirement Specification (SRS)

Definition: A Software Requirement Specification is a formal document that describes the software system's functional requirements, non-functional requirements, interfaces and constraints.

Purpose of an SRS

  • Creates a common understanding between customer and developer.
  • Defines the scope and boundaries of the system.
  • Acts as a foundation for design, coding, testing and maintenance.
  • Supports project estimation, planning and change control.
  • Provides a basis for acceptance testing.

Characteristics of a good SRS

CharacteristicMeaning
CorrectEvery requirement represents an actual stakeholder need.
UnambiguousEach requirement has only one interpretation.
CompleteAll required functions, constraints and responses are included.
ConsistentNo requirement contradicts another.
VerifiableEach requirement can be checked by inspection, analysis, demonstration or testing.
ModifiableChanges can be made without introducing confusion or inconsistency.
TraceableEach requirement can be traced to its source and forward to design, code or tests.
RankedRequirements are prioritised by importance or stability.

Typical SRS structure

  1. Introduction: purpose, scope, definitions, references and document overview.
  2. Overall description: product perspective, product functions, user classes, operating environment, constraints and assumptions.
  3. Specific requirements: functional requirements, external interface requirements, performance requirements, logical database requirements, design constraints and quality attributes.
  4. Appendices and supporting information: glossary, models, references and supplementary details.
Writing tip: Prefer measurable statements. “The system shall be fast” is not verifiable; “The system shall display the dashboard within 3 seconds for 95% of requests under the specified load” is testable.

02.7 Use Cases and UML

Use case: A description of how an external actor interacts with a system to achieve a particular goal.

Actor: a person, organisation, device or external system that interacts with the system. An actor represents a role, not necessarily one specific person.

Use case diagram: a UML diagram showing actors, use cases and their relationships within a system boundary.

Example: Library management system

Conceptual actor-to-use-case view. This simplified flowchart-style representation illustrates interactions; a formal UML use-case diagram uses stick-figure actors, ovals and a system boundary.

Elements and relationships

  • Actor: external role interacting with the system.
  • Use case: goal-oriented service provided by the system.
  • System boundary: defines what is inside the system being modelled.
  • Association: communication link between an actor and a use case.
  • <<include>>: required reuse of another use case's behaviour.
  • <<extend>>: optional or conditional behaviour extending a base use case at an extension point.

Use case description template

FieldDescription
Use case nameShort goal-oriented name.
Primary actorActor initiating the interaction.
PreconditionsConditions that must be true before the use case starts.
Main success scenarioNormal sequence of actor-system interactions.
Alternative / exception flowsOther paths, including error conditions.
PostconditionsState of the system after completion.

02.8 Scenario-Based Modelling

Scenario-based modelling describes the system from the viewpoint of users and other external actors. It explains how users achieve goals through interactions with the proposed system.

Scenario example: Borrow a library book

  1. The member signs in to the library system.
  2. The member searches for a book.
  3. The system displays matching books and availability.
  4. The member selects an available book and requests a borrow.
  5. The system checks membership status and borrowing limits.
  6. If eligible, the system records the loan and displays the due date.

Scenario flow

Scenario model components

  • Actors: users or external entities.
  • Goals: outcomes actors want to achieve.
  • Preconditions: conditions required before the scenario begins.
  • Main flow: normal interaction sequence.
  • Alternative flows: branches for optional, exceptional or failure conditions.
  • Postconditions: outcomes after the scenario completes.

02.9 Class-Based Modelling

Class-based modelling represents the static structure of a system by identifying classes, their attributes, operations and relationships.

Main elements

  • Class: a blueprint describing objects with common attributes and behaviour.
  • Attribute: data maintained by a class.
  • Operation: a service or behaviour provided by a class.
  • Association: a structural relationship between classes.
  • Multiplicity: indicates how many objects participate in a relationship.
  • Inheritance / generalisation: a specialised class inherits features from a general class.

Example: Library domain class model

A member can have multiple loan records over time; each loan record refers to one member and one book.

Steps in class-based modelling

  1. Identify candidate classes from the problem description.
  2. Identify attributes and operations for each class.
  3. Determine associations and multiplicities.
  4. Identify generalisation and whole-part relationships where applicable.
  5. Review the model for missing, redundant or incorrectly assigned responsibilities.

02.10 Behavioural Modelling

Behavioural modelling describes how a system or object behaves in response to events. It focuses on states, transitions, events and the sequence of interactions over time.

State machine model example: Book loan

Illustrative states and transitions for a book's circulation status. A production model may add states such as lost, damaged or under maintenance.

Behavioural modelling concepts

  • State: a condition of an object at a particular time.
  • Event: an occurrence that may trigger a change of state.
  • Transition: movement from one state to another due to an event and, where applicable, a guard condition.
  • Action: activity performed during a transition or while in a state.

Why behavioural models are useful

  • Clarify event-driven and state-dependent system behaviour.
  • Reveal missing transitions and exceptional cases.
  • Support design and test-case development.
  • Help stakeholders understand dynamic system behaviour.
