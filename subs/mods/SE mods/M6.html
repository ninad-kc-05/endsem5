06.1 Software Risk

Definition: Software risk is an expectation of loss or a potential problem that may or may not occur in the future. It can arise from lack of information, control or time. Loss may include increased production cost, poor-quality software or failure to complete a project on time.

Risk exists because the future is uncertain and known as well as unknown events may affect the project. Risk management aims to identify possible problems early and plan how to reduce their impact.

06.2 Types of Software Risks

1. Schedule Risk

Time-related or delivery-related risk caused by an incorrect schedule. It can delay development and delivery and may lead to project failure.

Causes:

  • Incorrect time estimation.
  • Improper resource allocation or tracking.
  • Frequent scope expansion.
  • Failure to identify and complete functions.

2. Budget Risk

Financial risk, mainly caused by budget overruns or poor financial management.

Causes:

  • Incorrect budget estimation.
  • Unexpected scope expansion.
  • Mismanagement of funds.
  • Cost overruns and poor budget tracking.

3. Operational Risk

Procedural risk arising during day-to-day project activities due to poor process implementation or operational issues.

Causes:

  • Insufficient resources or skilled staff.
  • Task and employee conflicts.
  • Poor task management and planning.
  • Lack of communication, role clarity or training.

4. Technical Risk

Functional or performance risk associated with the software product or its implementation.

Causes:

  • Frequent requirement changes.
  • Complex implementation.
  • Insufficient technical skills.
  • Improper module integration.

5. Programmatic Risk

External or unavoidable risk that originates outside the project team's direct control.

Causes:

  • Rapid market changes.
  • Insufficient or exhausted funds.
  • Changes in government rules or policies.
  • Loss of contracts.
Another classification used in risk identification in the notes: technology risks, people risks, organizational risks, tools risks, requirement risks and estimation risks. These categories classify risks by their source, whereas the five types above describe common project risk areas.

06.3 Risk Assessment, Analysis and Control

Risk management activities

The notes describe risk identification, risk analysis/assessment, risk control, risk planning and risk monitoring as key activities.

1. Risk Identification

Anticipate possible risks as early as possible. Identify significant risks and classify them by source so that appropriate management plans can be prepared.

2. Risk Analysis / Assessment

For each identified risk, estimate its probability of occurring and the seriousness of its consequences. The supplied notes use probability bands and impact categories rather than claiming that exact numerical estimates are always possible.

Probability bandRangeEffect / severity categories
Very low0–10%Catastrophic – threatens the survival of the plan
Serious – causes significant delays
Tolerable – delay is within allowed contingency
Insignificant – negligible effect
Low10–25%
Moderate25–50%
High50–75%
Very highAbove 75%
Risk Priority (p) = Probability (r) × Severity (s)

Here, p is the priority for controlling the risk, r is the probability of occurrence and s is the severity of loss. Risks with higher priority should receive attention first.

Illustrative calculation: If a risk has probability 0.6 and severity 8 on the chosen severity scale, then p = 0.6 × 8 = 4.8. This is an illustrative use of the formula; the notes do not prescribe a universal severity scale.

3. Risk Control

  • Avoid the risk: change the plan or requirements to remove the source of risk where possible.
  • Transfer the risk: assign the risky element to a third party or use insurance where appropriate.
  • Risk reduction: take steps to reduce the likelihood or loss, such as arranging backup staff.

Risk Leverage

Risk leverage compares the reduction in risk exposure with the cost of reducing the risk.

Risk Leverage = (Risk exposure before reduction − Risk exposure after reduction) / Cost of reduction

4. Risk Planning and 5. Risk Monitoring

Risk planning develops ways to handle each key risk. Risk monitoring checks whether assumptions about product, process and business risks have changed and whether predicted risks are occurring.

06.4 RMMM – Risk Mitigation, Monitoring and Management

Definition: RMMM is a plan included in the software project plan that documents how identified risks will be mitigated, monitored and managed if they occur. The notes also mention documenting risks in a Risk Information Sheet (RIS), which can be maintained in a database for searching, prioritization and analysis.

Risk Mitigation

Activities performed to avoid or reduce the causes of risk before they create problems.

  • Identify the risk.
  • Remove or reduce its causes.
  • Control relevant documents regularly.
  • Conduct timely reviews.

Risk Monitoring

Tracks the project and checks whether risk assumptions and mitigation actions remain valid.

  • Check whether predicted risks occur.
  • Ensure risk-avoidance steps are applied.
  • Collect data for future risk analysis.
  • Trace problems to the risks that caused them.

Risk Management

Plans the response when mitigation fails and the risk becomes reality. The project manager applies contingency actions and records the response in the risk register.

RMMM example – High staff turnover

RMMM componentActions for staff-turnover risk
MitigationMeet staff to understand reasons for turnover; address controllable causes; distribute project knowledge; establish documentation standards; assign backup staff for critical roles.
MonitoringMonitor team attitude under project pressure, interpersonal relationships, compensation concerns and job availability inside and outside the company.
Management / contingencyIf staff leave, use backups and documentation, reallocate resources, adjust the schedule and allow replacement staff to become familiar with the work.

Limitations of RMMM

  • It increases project cost.
  • It requires additional time.
  • For large projects, implementing RMMM can itself become a substantial task.
  • It cannot guarantee a risk-free project; new risks may appear even after delivery.

06.5 Software Configuration Management (SCM)

Definition: Software Configuration Management is the process of systematically managing, organizing and controlling changes in documents, code and other software entities throughout the Software Development Life Cycle.

Why SCM is needed

  • Multiple developers work on software that changes continuously.
  • Several versions, branches and authors may exist, often across geographically distributed teams.
  • Changes in requirements, policies, budget and schedule must be accommodated.
  • Software may need to run on different machines and operating systems.
  • SCM improves coordination and helps control the cost of changes.

SCM process / activities

1. Configuration identification and establishment

Identify configuration items (CIs), establish relationships among them, define control levels and establish change-management procedures. A baseline is a formally reviewed and agreed set of mutually consistent configuration items that becomes the basis for further development.

2. Version control

Create and maintain versions of configuration objects so that new products can be built from existing versions. The version history example in the notes shows minor updates such as 1.1.1 and 1.1.2, later versions such as 1.2, 1.3 and 1.4, and a major evolutionary path such as 2.0. More than one version may remain supported.

3. Change control

Review, authorize, document, implement and test requested changes in a controlled manner.

06.6 Version Control and Change Control

Version Control

A version control system records changes to source-code elements and other configuration objects, including who made a change, when it was made and why. It supports collaboration, history tracking and reversal of changes when necessary.

Capabilities listed in the notes:

  • Project repository: stores relevant configuration objects.
  • Version management: stores versions and enables a past version to be reconstructed.
  • Make facility: collects relevant configuration objects to construct a specific software version.
  • Issue / bug tracking: records and tracks outstanding issues for configuration objects.

Change Control

Change control is a systematic approach to managing changes so that unnecessary changes are avoided, all changes are documented, services are not disrupted unnecessarily and resources are used efficiently.

StageAction
1. Change request initiationStandardize the request, submit it for management review and keep the requester informed.
2. Impact assessmentAssess the possible impact of the requested change in a structured way.
3. Control and documentationMaintain a change log with date, person and details of the change; restrict changes to authorized individuals.
4. Documentation and proceduresUpdate procedures and associated documents when system changes are implemented.
5. Authorized maintenance and testingControl access rights and thoroughly test the software.
6. Version control and emergency changesEnsure the latest approved version is updated. Emergency changes may receive verbal authorization but must be documented as soon as possible.

06.7 Software Quality Management

Definition: Software Quality Management ensures that the required level of quality is achieved by improving the product development process. Quality is treated as everyone's responsibility.

The supplied notes state that quality management should be independent of project management to maintain independence from cost and schedule pressures. It directly affects process quality and indirectly affects product quality.

Quality Assurance (QA)

Develops organizational procedures and standards for quality at the organizational level.

Quality Planning

Selects applicable procedures and standards for a project and modifies them as required to prepare a quality plan.

Quality Control

Checks that the development team follows the selected practices and standards to produce quality software.

06.8 Software Quality Assurance (SQA)

Definition: SQA is a set of activities for ensuring quality in software processes and checking that the developed software conforms to defined or standardized quality requirements. It is an ongoing activity throughout the SDLC, not merely a final check after development.

SQA checks quality during each development phase. The process moves to the next phase only when the current phase meets the required quality standards.

Components of an SQA system (as listed in the notes)

  1. Pre-project components: ensure that resources, schedule and budget are available; define quality and development plans; and establish a shared understanding of quality between the developer and customer.
  2. Project life-cycle activity assessment: assess development and maintenance activities. Development-phase activities include reviews, expert opinions and software testing. Maintenance activities and work performed by subcontractors or external contributors are also assessed.
  3. Infrastructure error prevention and improvement: reduce or eliminate recurring errors across the organization.
  4. Software quality management: control development and maintenance activities and provide administrative support to prevent schedule and budget failures.
  5. Standardization, certification and SQA system assessment: use recognized professional standards, improve coordination with other organizations and assess the quality system against common standards. The notes distinguish quality-management standards and project-process standards.
  6. Organizing for SQA – human components: bring together managers, testing staff, SQA trustees/committees and SQA group members to implement SQA, identify deviations and propose improvements.

06.10 Software Maintenance

Definition: Software maintenance is the modification of software after delivery to correct faults, adapt it to a changed environment, improve it or prevent future problems.
Source coverage note: The supplied Unit 6 chapter notes identify maintenance in the unit title and mention maintenance activities within SQA, but do not provide a detailed explanation of maintenance types in the extracted chapter text. The four categories below are standard supplementary explanations, not a verbatim expansion from those notes.

Corrective Maintenance

Changes made to correct faults or errors discovered after delivery.

Example: fixing an incorrect invoice total.

Adaptive Maintenance

Changes made to keep software usable in a changed environment.

Example: updating software to work with a new operating system or database version.

Perfective Maintenance

Changes made to improve performance, usability or functionality in response to user needs.

Example: adding a report filter requested by users.

Preventive Maintenance

Changes made to improve maintainability and reduce the likelihood of future failures.

Example: refactoring complex code and improving documentation.
